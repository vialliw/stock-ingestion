**Introduction: Interpreting Cross-Asset Correlations — Yields, Oil, Equities & Semiconductors**

These charts examine the evolving relationships among five key market variables:

- **^TNX** — the 10-year U.S. Treasury yield (a benchmark long-term discount rate and risk-free rate proxy)  
- **WTI (CL=F)** — the price of West Texas Intermediate crude oil  
- **^IRX** — the 13-week Treasury bill yield (a short-term rate proxy closely linked to Fed policy and the discount rate)  
- **SPY** — the S&P 500  
- **SMH** — the semiconductor sector (a high-duration, growth-sensitive segment of equities)

### Why these relationships matter

Interest rates and oil prices are two of the most powerful macro forces affecting asset valuations and economic conditions. Higher yields raise the discount rate applied to future cash flows, which typically pressures equity valuations—especially for long-duration growth assets such as semiconductors. Simultaneously, oil price moves influence inflation expectations, which in turn feed back into yields and monetary policy. Understanding how tightly (or loosely) these variables move together helps investors anticipate regime shifts, assess risk, and interpret market pricing of growth, inflation, and policy.

### What to look for in the charts

1. **Correlation Heatmaps (Levels vs. Daily Returns)**  
   Levels correlations capture long-term co-movement driven by shared trends (e.g., the post-2020 recovery and the 2022–2023 rate-hike cycle). Returns correlations reveal short-term, day-to-day linkages. A large gap between the two often signals that co-movement is trend-driven rather than reactive. Watch especially for the strength of the SMH–SPY link (typically very high) and the more variable oil–yield and equity–yield relationships.

2. **Normalized Price Chart (Base = 100)**  
   This view shows relative performance and the magnitude of moves. Note the dramatic divergence of SMH versus the broader market and the multi-year rise in both short- and long-term yields after 2021. Large directional swings in yields or oil that coincide with equity drawdowns or rallies are particularly informative.

3. **Multi-Panel Overlays**  
   These dual-axis charts make the timing of moves explicit. Look for periods when oil and the 10-year yield rise or fall in tandem (inflation/geopolitical risk transmission), when equities sell off as yields rise (classic discount-rate pressure), and how the short-rate–long-rate relationship evolves (steepening or flattening of the curve).

4. **Rolling Correlation (default 60-day window)**  
   This is the most dynamic view. Correlations are rarely stable. Watch for:
   - Spikes in oil–yield correlation during geopolitical or inflation shocks  
   - Episodes of negative equity–yield correlation (risk-off or growth-scare regimes)  
   - Persistent high SMH–SPY correlation (sector leadership or broad risk appetite)  
   Sudden breaks in these relationships often mark important regime changes.

5. **Scatter Plots**  
   These reveal the overall shape of the relationship and any non-linearities or clusters of observations. Tight linear patterns indicate stable co-movement; dispersed or curved clouds suggest changing regimes or structural breaks.

Taken together, the charts illustrate how the discount rate, energy prices, and equity markets interact across different market environments. The interactive version allows you to vary the look-back window and date range so you can test the stability of these relationships yourself.
